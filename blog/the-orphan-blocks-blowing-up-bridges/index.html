The orphan (blocks) blowing up bridges

Oct 7, 2026 | Bitfinding Research Team

While investigating Ethereum reorgs, we tested whether Across relayers would pay for deposits in blocks that later disappeared. Three distinct relayers filled 15 of our deposits, sending 2.9986275 WETH (wrapped ETH) on Robinhood Chain. Ethereum discarded the source blocks, but the destination payments remained.

A one-block reorg was enough. Here’s how an ordinary blockchain event can leave a fast bridge relayer paying for a deposit that no longer exists, and how Ethereum’s Fast Confirmation Rule could help.

What is an orphan block?

Ethereum’s latest blocks are still subject to change. A block may arrive late, or validators may see competing branches. The network uses fork choice to select the branch with the greatest weight of validator votes, called attestations. Replacing a previously accepted branch is a chain reorganization, or reorg.

We call a discarded block an orphan block. It can contain valid, successfully executed transactions and briefly appear as the latest block. When Ethereum chooses another branch, the orphan’s state changes and events are removed from the canonical chain: the history the network keeps.

Imagine a bridge deposit in that block. Your tokens were transferred and the contract emitted a deposit event. After the reorg, that deposit has disappeared from Ethereum’s accepted history. The block wasn’t necessarily invalid; it lost fork choice. Ethereum’s consensus documentation explains how these votes select the chain.

Why RPCs distribute these blocks

An RPC lets an application read a node’s current view of the chain. If a block becomes that node’s head, the RPC can return its transactions, receipts, and events, even if the block will later be orphaned. Seeing a successful transaction through an RPC doesn’t guarantee that it will stay in the chain.

This is expected behavior. Geth’s subscription documentation says a reorg can produce different headers at the same height. Events from the discarded branch are sent again with removed: true.

A removal lets an indexer delete a database row, but can’t undo money sent on another chain. Several RPCs can also report the same temporary block; their agreement doesn’t create confirmation.

What happened in our test

Across makes transfers fast by having relayers advance their own capital. Its documented lifecycle has three steps:

  1. The user deposits tokens on the source chain.
  2. A relayer observes the deposit and pays the recipient on the destination chain using its own tokens.
  3. A later settlement process matches the fill to the source deposit and repays eligible relayers.

The relayer decides how much source confirmation to require before step 2. Our first batch shows the risk of acting on the latest block. Ethereum block 25,658,830 contained five deposits. All five received successful destination fills, including this payment. The fill block’s timestamp is five seconds after the orphan’s timestamp and seven seconds before the replacement block’s timestamp.

Ethereum then kept a history without those deposits. The canonical replacement transaction contains none of them. Robinhood Chain kept the payments.

Across six batches of five deposits, three batches were fully filled and two received no fills. The sixth transaction was included again on canonical Ethereum, received no fills, and was later refunded.

Each batch shared one transaction and one reorg outcome. All six rows start with a losing source branch; the chart doesn’t estimate an attack’s success rate.

Another batch’s fills were timestamped 33–42 seconds after the orphan. These records don’t reveal each operator’s confirmation settings or when it noticed the reorg: block timestamps aren’t local observation times.

The total 2.9986275 WETH is gross orphan exposure, plus destination gas. We haven’t established the final loss after any recovery or reimbursement. The payments came from relayer inventory; settlement still requires matching canonical deposits. Across identifies finality risk as a relayer risk.

A reorg can also roll back a deposit counter. Compare full order parameters: an unrelated deposit can later reuse the missing deposit’s ID.

The Upbit pattern

Ethereum organizes time into 12-second slots and 32-slot epochs. Slot 0 is the first slot of an epoch. A 21-month study on Ethereum Research found an orphan rate of 1.17% at slot 0, compared with 0.16% across the other positions.

A validator cluster labeled Upbit stood out: 31% of its slot-0 proposals were orphaned, accounting for about 18% of all slot-0 orphans. Around 97% of its orphaned slot-0 blocks were built locally. The label is a public cluster attribution; these historical rates don’t predict a particular block.

Left: orphan rates. Right: shares of slot-0 orphans. The denominators differ.

This gives us a useful heuristic for investigating reorg risk: epoch-boundary proposals, late publication, and local building often appeared together.

One possible contributor is the validator’s block-building policy. MEV-Boost obtains builder bids through relays. Its min-bid setting sets a floor: if no bid qualifies, the validator falls back to building locally. A 2024 Data Always analysis inferred Upbit’s use of this setting from payment distributions.

The study’s discussion suggests that waiting for bids before building locally could contribute to late blocks. Operator logs would be needed to confirm the cause. Builder relays and Across’s payment relayers have different jobs.

Why the deposit might never come back

After a reorg, a transaction may return to the pending pool and be included again. But successful execution in one block doesn’t guarantee successful execution in a later block.

A contract can use block.timestamp to check whether an operation’s validity window is still open. If that window has closed by the next block, the same call reverts instead of recreating the deposit. The transaction can still be valid under Ethereum’s rules and consume gas; its contract operation fails.

That’s why a relayer can’t assume a removed deposit will eventually return. A source execution deadline also doesn’t reverse an existing destination payment. Our experiment used replacement transactions; timestamp expiry is an additional failure mode, rather than a result demonstrated by these batches.

Fast confirmation without waiting for finality

Ethereum finality normally takes about 13 minutes. Fast bridges compete on seconds. The Fast Confirmation Rule (FCR) offers an intermediate check: use validator votes to assess whether a block has enough support to remain in the eventual finalized chain.

FCR runs in a consensus client without a hard fork. It considers fork-choice votes and competing branches, so a weak block can remain unconfirmed even when execution RPCs are distributing it. Its guarantees require timely communication and less than 25% adversarial stake. Extreme network conditions can still cause a reorg; FCR doesn’t provide the same slashable-stake protection as finality.

EthPandaOps’ March 2026 mainnet study used a simplified same-slot measure: about 95% of the slot’s committee voting for the head. Across more than a million slots, 96.9% qualified; none of 1,900 observed orphan blocks reached that threshold. A small live sample reached it at a median of about 8.1 seconds after slot start.

That proxy isn’t the full FCR algorithm. A later replay across four client implementations reported zero false confirmations across 5.15 million evaluations over those clients. About 96% of slots in the comparable window confirmed within 12 seconds. These are measured results, not a guarantee for every network condition.

For relayers, the useful change is to check consensus support before paying. Require confirmation of the exact source block containing the deposit, and keep waiting when the evidence is missing or inconsistent. Check what an RPC provider’s safe tag means before relying on it; FCR can change its semantics. Also discard queued work when its source block is removed.

These results support using FCR to filter weak branches while keeping well-supported deposits fast. They don’t demonstrate a fix for our own fills without testing the relayer integration.

Rare reorgs still matter

Upbit accounted for about 18% of slot-0 orphans in the study, leaving roughly 82% elsewhere. Reorgs also happen at other epoch positions. Fixing one validator operator won’t remove the need for source confirmation.

Nor does an attack need frequent reorgs to be economically attractive. An actor taking advantage of an ordinary reorg doesn’t pay to reorganize Ethereum. Occasional destination payouts can outweigh the costs of other attempts, depending on fees, accepted order sizes, liquidity, and recovery. Our experiment demonstrates the risk, without establishing a profitable strategy or a general success probability.

One unmatched payment can put the entire advanced amount at risk against many fills earning small fees. Deposits in the same source block share its reorg risk, so exposure limits should account for them together.

At Bitfinding, we investigate how these failures reach applications. For teams building fast bridges, source confirmation belongs in the payment decision. A successful deposit receipt needs enough consensus support before it justifies spending money on another chain.
