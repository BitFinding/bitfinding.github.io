The orphan (blocks) blowing up bridges

Oct 7, 2026 | Bitfinding Research Team

A bridge deposit appeared on Ethereum. Relayers paid out on another chain. Then Ethereum removed the block containing the deposit.

The payments stayed.

In a bounded mainnet experiment, we observed three distinct Across relayers deliver 2.9986275 WETH across 15 fills for deposits first recorded in Ethereum blocks that later lost their place in the chain. Each reorg was only one block deep. The destination payments survived independently.

This is what happens when a fast bridge spends money on the strength of a block that is still provisional. To understand the failure, we need to follow the same event through three systems: Ethereum consensus, an RPC feed, and a relayer willing to advance capital.

What is an orphan block?

A blockchain’s recent history can change. Two valid blocks may compete, or a late block may arrive after many validators have already voted for another head. Ethereum’s fork choice decides which branch the network follows, using the weight of validator votes, called attestations.

An orphan block, as we use the term here, is a block that was observed and may briefly have been accepted as the head, but was later excluded from the canonical chain: the history the network ultimately keeps. You may also see these called stale or reorged blocks. This usage differs from the older meaning of an orphan as a block whose parent is unknown.

The block can be perfectly valid. Its transactions executed correctly against its parent. Losing fork choice removes those state changes and events from canonical history. A successful receipt from that temporary branch therefore does not establish that the payment will survive.

Ethereum distinguishes its changing head from finality, which is backed by votes representing at least two-thirds of staked ETH. Reverting finalized history requires a major consensus failure and slashable stake. Ordinary head reorgs happen before that protection applies. See Ethereum’s consensus documentation.

Two chains make separate decisions. Replacing a source block does not undo a payment already committed on the destination.

Your RPC will show you the block that disappears

An RPC is the interface applications use to read a node’s view of the chain. When an application asks for the latest block, subscribes to new heads, or listens for contract events, it receives information about that node’s current head. That head can later be replaced.

RPCs distribute blocks and events that will later be orphaned. They do not know the future when they send them. Returning a block says that the node has accepted it in its present view; it does not promise permanent inclusion.

Geth documents this explicitly: a head subscription can emit different headers at the same height, and a log subscription resends events from a discarded branch with removed: true. Geth’s subscription documentation describes these as normal reorg behavior.

For an indexer, a removal can undo a database row. For a relayer that has already paid on another chain, the notification arrives after the consequential decision. Ethereum cannot reverse that destination transaction.

Reading the same block from several RPC providers improves resilience to individual provider faults. It does not create consensus confirmation: all of those providers can report the same temporary branch.

The bridge pays before settlement catches up

Across is an intent bridge. The user deposits on the source chain, and a relayer supplies its own tokens on the destination chain. A later settlement process verifies the fill against the corresponding source deposit and repays eligible relayers. That separation is how users receive tokens quickly. Across’s lifecycle documentation explains the three phases.

The critical question is how much source confirmation the relayer requires before spending its inventory. In our experiment, responding relayers acted on deposits in the provisional Ethereum head without waiting for another source block to establish their survival. Across itself documents finality risk for relayers.

The canonical replacement transaction contains none of those deposits. Across’s later accounting needs a matching canonical deposit; the orphan’s event cannot supply one.

We observed six five-deposit batches on losing source branches. Three were fully filled, two received no fills, and one transaction was later included in canonical Ethereum, received no fills, and was refunded.

The six branches are the units of observation. Five deposits in one batch shared one source transaction and one fork outcome. All six batches are conditioned on an initial source reorg; this is not a success-rate estimate.

We call the 2.9986275 WETH gross orphan exposure, plus destination gas. Public receipts do not establish the final unrecovered loss after any private reimbursement or recovery. They also do not establish a drain of Across’s settlement pools. The observed money came from relayer inventory.

There is a forensic trap here too. A reorg can roll back a deposit counter, allowing an unrelated canonical deposit to reuse the same number. Settlement must compare the complete order parameters. Matching a numeric deposit ID alone can make an orphan fill appear backed by the wrong deposit.

Why Upbit kept appearing in the investigation

Ethereum groups 12-second slots into 32-slot epochs. Slot 0 is the first slot of an epoch. A 21-month study published on Ethereum Research found a 1.17% orphan rate there, versus 0.16% across other positions.

A cluster labeled Upbit orphaned 31% of its own slot-0 proposals and accounted for approximately 18% of all slot-0 orphans. Around 97% of its orphaned slot-0 blocks were locally built. These are historical statistics and public operator labels, rather than a prediction or proof of legal ownership.

Rounded figures from the linked study. The left panel compares orphan rates; the right compares shares of slot-0 orphans. Their denominators differ.

The resulting Upbit heuristic is an observation about risk: epoch-boundary proposals, late arrival, and local building appeared together unusually often. It gives investigators a pattern to examine; it cannot certify that a block will disappear.

There is a plausible link to block-building policy. MEV-Boost lets validators obtain blocks through builder relays. Its minimum-bid setting rejects builder bids below an operator’s chosen floor. When none qualifies, the validator falls back to building locally. An earlier Data Always analysis inferred Upbit’s use of this policy from payment distributions, while warning that the result was a market-dependent snapshot.

Waiting for bids and then taking a slower local path could contribute to late publication. That is an interaction hypothesis, not a confirmed explanation for every block. MEV-Boost’s builder relays and a bridge’s payment relayers also have different jobs; the shared word does not make them the same system.

A transaction can lose its chance to succeed

After a reorg, a transaction may return to the pending pool and be included again. A disappearing block therefore does not guarantee a permanently disappearing deposit.

But a transaction’s execution can depend on time. Solidity exposes the source block’s onchain clock as block.timestamp. A contract may accept an operation while its validity window is open and revert the same call once that window has expired. If the next block is outside that window, re-execution cannot recreate the successful deposit.

This is the distinction behind “making the transaction invalid in the next block.” The signed transaction can remain valid under Ethereum’s transaction rules; the contract operation fails at the later timestamp. It may still consume gas if included. A source execution deadline is also distinct from a bridge’s destination fill deadline: expiring one does not undo a payment on the other chain.

For defenders, the implication is direct. “It will probably be included again” is an unsafe basis for credit. Time-sensitive operations may never recreate the event that justified the payout. Our linked experiment used canonical replacement transactions; this timestamp case explains an additional failure mode, rather than claiming a separately demonstrated result.

Fast confirmation can close much of this gap

Waiting for finality offers strong protection, but it takes roughly 13 minutes under normal conditions. Fast bridges compete on seconds. There is a useful intermediate approach: Ethereum’s Fast Confirmation Rule, or FCR.

FCR uses validator attestations to decide whether a block has sufficient support to remain in the eventual finalized chain under its assumptions. It evaluates the fork-choice view, including competing branches and vote weight. That adds consensus evidence before the application releases money.

The rule runs locally in a consensus client and does not require a hard fork. Its guarantees assume timely communication and less than 25% adversarial stake. It provides a different guarantee from slashable-stake finality; extreme network conditions can still produce a reorg. The FCR project documentation explains those assumptions and fallback behavior.

Confirmation policy determines when destination capital becomes eligible to move. A fast path needs a waiting path when its evidence is insufficient.

The measured evidence is encouraging. EthPandaOps’ March 2026 mainnet investigation examined 1,074,543 slots using a same-slot vote-ratio proxy. At approximately 95% committee support, 96.9% of slots qualified. None of 1,900 observed orphan blocks reached that threshold. A separate 372-slot live sample crossed it at a median of about 8.1 seconds after slot start.

Those numbers describe a simplified proxy, rather than the full algorithm. The later 12-month replay across four client implementations tested the actual implementations: across 5.15 million slot evaluations, it reported zero false confirmations. Roughly 96% of slots in the comparable window confirmed within 12 seconds. Client differences and imperfect replay data remain reasons to validate the implementation and observation path.

For a bridge relayer, the policy is straightforward: make the exact source block eligible for a fill only after the confirmation check accepts it. Insufficient or inconsistent evidence should keep the order waiting for a stronger source state. An RPC quorum or an elapsed timer cannot substitute for that check. Operators using a provider’s safe tag should establish what that provider actually means by it, since FCR deployment can change its semantics.

FCR could help reject the weak branches behind this class of incident while preserving fast settlement for well-supported blocks. The published studies support that direction; they do not prove that an untested relayer integration would have prevented every fill in our experiment.

Upbit is a warning, not the boundary of the problem

The same study places approximately 82% of slot-0 orphans outside the Upbit-associated cluster. Reorgs also occur at other epoch positions. Fixing one operator’s setup would reduce one concentration of failures; applications would still need to handle provisional source state.

A rare reorg can remain economically attractive to abuse because the actor benefiting from it need not pay to reorganize Ethereum. The network can produce the branch change through ordinary operation. The economic question then becomes whether occasional unmatched destination payments outweigh the costs incurred along the way. Rarity alone does not settle that question.

For a relayer, this creates an uneven trade: many ordinary fills earn small fees, while one orphaned batch can put the entire advanced principal at risk. Five orders in the same source block share that risk. Exposure limits should therefore account for their common block and route, as well as each order’s size. A low average orphan rate can coexist with an unacceptable loss tail.

Our experiment establishes the possibility of early payments surviving a source reorg. It does not establish a general attack probability, profitable scale, or behavior across other bridge protocols. Each system’s confirmation policy, acceptance limits, liquidity, costs, and recovery paths change the economics. Across was the protocol tested here.

The architectural lesson is that a source event is still provisional until the application’s confirmation policy has accepted the block containing it. Crossing to another chain makes that distinction expensive: the deposit can disappear after the money has already arrived.
